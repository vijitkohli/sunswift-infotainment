## 1. How a race-car infotainment system differs from a normal mobile app

A mobile app can generally assume sustained attention and allow a user to pause or inspect an error. A race-car display receives only brief glances while the driver controls the vehicle. It must therefore present essential information with low, predictable latency and remain responsive as telemetry updates continuously. 
It is also often operated under glare, vibration, and heat. Error tolerance is much lower in the case of a race-car infotainment display: a delayed interaction in a mobile app may be a small inconvenience, while a misleading vehicle reading could negatively influence a safety-critical decision.

## 2. Four design principles

**Attention proportional to consequence.** Visual size and importance should reflect the consequence of missing information. A critical motor-temperature warning should take priority over routine status information. 
If every value receives equal emphasis, the driver may recognise a dangerous condition too late.

**Robust degradation under unreliable telemetry.** Dropped or corrupted readings require defined fallback behaviour. A previous value may be retained briefly where appropriate, but it must expire and become unavailable rather than remain indefinitely or appear as zero. Holding a predicted or last measured temperature reading for long could conceal a developing thermal fault.

**Design around known blind spots.** The interface must not imply certainty that the available sensors cannot provide. For example, with only the available source, a speed sensor stuck at zero may be indistinguishable from a stationary car. Estimated and recovered values should therefore be labelled explicitly instead of displayed in the same way we would with accurate readings. Otherwise, the driver may trust a reading more than the evidence warrants.

**Predictability over polish.** Important values should retain fixed positions, units, and warning behaviour. Stable placement lets the driver locate information through muscle memory instead of manually searching each time. 
In racing conditions, the driver only has time for a brief glace. If values move or warnings behave inconsistently, that brief glance may not be enough to make an informed decision.

Together, these principles protect the driver’s limited attention while preserving trust in the display.

## 3. Testing the interface

I would test the interface in layers, beginning with automated tests for parsing, state transitions, warning behaviour, and missing or invalid values. Recorded and generated telemetry would then be replayed at normal, burst, and sustained high update rates while injecting dropouts, delays, corruption, duplication, and out-of-order messages. During stress and soak tests, I would monitor input-to-display latency, frame times, dropped frames, freezes, CPU and memory use, and recovery after faults.

The complete display hardware should also be tested under representative heat, vibration, glare, and power interruptions, since correct software may still fail in its operating environment. Driver trials should measure how quickly essential values, warnings, and unavailable or estimated states can be recognised during brief glances without unnecessary distraction. 
Finally, raw inputs, normalised values, quality-state changes, warnings, and system faults should be timestamped and logged for diagnosis and replay. Passing these tests would reduce known risks, but physical testing and driver validation would still be required.
